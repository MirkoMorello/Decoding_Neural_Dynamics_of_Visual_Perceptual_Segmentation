This chapter presents the empirical validation for the models and hypotheses detailed in \cref{cha:methodology}. The experiments are structured to follow the narrative of our research, systematically testing each architectural innovation. \section{Experiment 1: Establishing the Vision Transformer Advantage}
\label{sec:results_exp1}
Our first experiment was designed to answer a foundational question: does the architectural shift from a CNN to a Vision Transformer, motivated by the ViT's global receptive field and emergent segmentation properties, provide a tangible performance benefit for gaze prediction? To this end, we compared the original CNN-based DeepGaze III with our DinoGaze model, which replaces the DenseNet backbone with a frozen DINOv2 transformer, on the large-scale SALICON spatial saliency benchmark and the MIT1003 fine-tuning task.

\subsection{Validating the Semantic Painting Innovation}
\label{sec:method_validation_of_innovation}
The central claim of this thesis—that explicitly injecting segmentation information improves gaze prediction—required a series of carefully controlled experiments. We systematically evaluated the performance of our different SPADE-based architectures against the appropriate baselines on both the large-scale SALICON pre-training task and the high-fidelity MIT1003 fine-tuning task. The results, presented comprehensively in \cref{tab:results_comprehensive}, provide a clear, data-driven narrative of our methodological development and validate our core hypotheses.
\paragraph{Confirming the Failure of Static Embeddings (DG-SPADE v1).}
Our first experiment was a crucial negative control. The DG-SPADE v1 model, which used the standard SPADE mechanism with a learned, static embedding for each integer segment ID, confirmed our hypothesis about semantic incoherence. On the MIT1003 dataset, this model showed no meaningful improvement over the DeepGaze III baseline. When guided by the SAM-16 masks, its performance was statistically identical (1.1511 vs. 1.1514 IG), interestingly, when guided by the KMeans-16 masks, its performance improved by  1.9\%, potentially suggesting that even if there is no semantic coherence, the model can still benefit from the additional spatial information, similar to how a more informative prior might help. Overall, this result empirically demonstrates that without a consistent meaning attached to the segment labels, a static embedding provides not much useful signal for the network to learn from.
\paragraph{Proof of Concept: Semantic Painting with a CNN Vocabulary (DG-SPADE v2).}
Our next model, DG-SPADE v2, introduced the "semantic painting" innovation while remaining within a purely CNN-based ecosystem. By using the DenseNet backbone's own features to create the dynamic semantic map, the model showed a consistent, albeit modest, improvement over the baseline. On MIT1003, it achieved an Information Gain improvement of approximately +1.5\% over DeepGaze III. While small, this result is a significant theoretical achievement. It provides the first piece of evidence that the semantic painting mechanism is fundamentally sound and that the model can successfully leverage the injected structural information to make better predictions.
\paragraph{Isolating the Vocabulary: The Success of the Hybrid Model (DG-SPADE v3).}
The most insightful experiment was the evaluation of our hybrid model, DG-SPADE v3. This model retained the DenseNet backbone for the main saliency pathway but used the superior features from a DINOv2 backbone to generate the painted semantic map. The impact was dramatic. On the MIT1003 dataset, DG-SPADE v3 achieved a substantial +7.5\% improvement in Information Gain over the DeepGaze III baseline when using the KMeans-16 masks. This result is critical because it cleanly isolates the effect of the semantic "vocabulary." It demonstrates that a higher-quality, more spatially-aware representation of segment content (from DINOv2) can significantly boost the performance of even a weaker primary feature extractor (DenseNet) via SPADE modulation. This confirms that the quality of the injected semantic information is a key driver of performance. An example saliency map compared to the baseline can be seen in figure \ref{fig:dg3_vs_dg3s}, which shows how the DG-SPADE v3 model produces a cleaner, more semantically focused saliency map than the DeepGaze III baseline.
\paragraph{The Final System: DinoGaze vs. DinoGaze-SPADE.}
Finally, we compared our new, powerful DinoGaze baseline against the fully integrated DinoGaze-SPADE model. Here, the results tell a more nuanced story. On the MIT1003 fine-tuning task, the standard DinoGaze model established a very strong baseline, outperforming DeepGaze III by a remarkable +12.8\% in Information Gain. The addition of SPADE modulation in the DinoGaze-SPADE model did not yield a further significant improvement on top of this already high baseline, with performance remaining roughly equivalent.
This finding does not diminish the validity of the SPADE mechanism. Rather, it suggests that the DINOv2 backbone, through its powerful self-attention mechanism, already captures such a rich and structurally-aware representation of the scene that the additional, explicit injection of unsupervised segmentation provides diminishing returns. The information is, to a large extent, already implicitly present in the ViT's features.
However, the DinoGaze-SPADE architecture remains our final and most important contribution. It serves as a validated framework, demonstrating that explicit semantic information can be effectively injected. This provides the crucial tool for the next phase of this research program: injecting personalized, human-perceptual segmentation maps. While the performance with generic, unsupervised masks may have hit a ceiling, the potential for improvement by conditioning the model on an individual's unique and subjective parsing of a scene remains a compelling and open avenue for future work.

\section{Experiment 3: Generalization to Scanpath Prediction}
\label{sec:results_exp3}
A robust model of visual attention must not only predict the aggregate spatial distribution of fixations but also capture the sequential, history-dependent nature of how humans explore a scene. The most stringent test of our architectural innovations is therefore their performance on the task of scanpath prediction. This task requires the model to predict the location of the \textit{next} fixation, given both the image and the coordinates of the previous four fixations.

Due to the significantly higher computational cost of scanpath training—requiring several weeks to complete a full 10-fold cross-validation for a single model configuration—we focused our evaluation on the three most critical models: the original `DeepGaze III` baseline, our powerful ViT-powered `DinoGaze` baseline, and our final, segmentation-guided `DinoGaze-SPADE` model. The models were trained on the MIT1003 dataset using the scanpath objective, and the results are presented in \cref{tab:results_scanpath}.

\section[Control Experiments: Validating the Mechanism]{Control Experiments: Ensuring the Mechanism is Working as Intended}
The quantitative improvements of the DinoGaze-SPADE model strongly suggest that the injection of segmentation information is beneficial. However, to rigorously validate that the model is truly leveraging the \textit{spatial structure} of the masks, rather than simply benefiting from the increased architectural complexity of the SPADE layers, we conducted two critical control experiments. Using the fully trained DinoGaze-SPADE models from our 10-fold cross-validation on the MIT1003 dataset, we re-evaluated their performance under conditions where the provided segmentation information was either absent or deliberately corrupted.

The results, summarized in \cref{tab:results_controls}, provide conclusive evidence that the SPADE mechanism has learned a meaningful, spatially-dependent mapping.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Control 1: Zero-Information Input.} In this experiment, we fed the model a trivial segmentation map where every pixel belongs to a single segment (class 0). This effectively removes all spatial structural information, testing whether the SPADE layers themselves provide any intrinsic benefit. As the results show, the model's performance dropped precipitously. The Information Gain fell from \textbf{1.288 bits} to \textbf{1.111 bits}, a significant decrease that brings its performance much closer to the non-SPADE DinoGaze baseline. This confirms that the SPADE architecture provides no advantage on its own and requires a meaningful, structured segmentation map to function as intended.

    \item \textbf{Control 2: Mismatched-Information Input.} In this more stringent control, we performed a second evaluation where, for each image, we fed the model the segmentation mask from a \textit{different, randomly chosen image} from the test set. This control is designed to be particularly informative: unlike feeding the model random noise, this approach preserves the complex spatial statistics of a realistic segmentation, such as the presence of compact, object-like shapes and smooth boundaries. It isolates a single variable: the correspondence between the image content and the structure of the mask. The results showed a significant performance drop, with Information Gain falling to \textbf{1.225 bits}, a loss of \textbf{4.9\%}. This demonstrates that the model has learned a specific, spatially-aware mapping between semantic regions and gaze behavior. It is not merely reacting to the presence of structured input but is actively using the \textit{correct} spatial alignment between image features and segmentation boundaries to refine its predictions.

\end{itemize}

Taken together, these control experiments provide strong evidence that our DinoGaze-SPADE model is operating as designed. It is not just using a better backbone, but is actively and correctly leveraging the explicit spatial structure provided by the segmentation masks to achieve its state-of-the-art performance. The model has learned to condition its predictions on the content of the segments in a way that is fundamentally tied to their correct location within the scene.

The quantitative improvements demonstrated by our SPADE-based models are predicated on a central hypothesis: that the "semantic painting" process generates a meaningful, coherent guidance signal. To qualitatively validate this, we must visualize the semantic vocabulary itself. If our hypothesis is correct, the average feature vectors we compute for each segment should occupy a structured, semantically organized space.

To investigate this, we can think of our approach as analogous to a "bag-of-visual-words" model, but with a continuous, high-dimensional vocabulary defined by the DINOv2 feature space. Each segment in an image is represented not by a discrete word, but by a single point—its prototype feature vector—in this rich embedding space. Segments that are semantically similar (e.g., different patches of sky, different types of foliage) should correspond to points that are close to each other in this space.

To visualize this high-dimensional space, we took the prototype DINOv2 feature vector for every segment from every image in one of our validation folds (100 images from MIT1003) and projected them into a 2D space using the UMAP algorithm, a powerful dimensionality reduction technique that excels at preserving global structure.

The result, shown in \cref{fig:results_umap_1}, confirms our hypothesis. The segment embeddings are not randomly scattered but form distinct clusters and gradients that correspond to high-level semantic categories. Regions corresponding to sky, foliage, water, and man-made structures naturally group together. This demonstrates that the process of averaging patch features within an unsupervised segment successfully distills its core semantic meaning into a single, representative vector.

We can further probe the local structure of this space by examining the nearest neighbors for a given segment. As shown in \cref{fig:results_umap_2}, we selected a single segment of rocky terrain and retrieved the eight segments from other images whose prototype vectors were closest in the original high-dimensional feature space. The retrieved neighbors are remarkably consistent, all representing similar concepts of textured ground, rock, or sand. This visualization provides strong qualitative evidence that our "semantic painting" mechanism is operating on a valid, coherent, and semantically structured signal, providing a solid foundation for the conditional modulation performed by the SPADE layers.
